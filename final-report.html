Rook Dispatch 4.2:
what went wrong, and how we fix it

Stacey Lavelle

Product Manager, Rook Dispatch

Rook Industries makes software for superheroes and the handlers who coordinate them. Rook Dispatch decides who gets pinged about an emergency callout, and whether they answer.

Release 4.2 shipped on 12 August and responders stopped answering. This report covers what I found, the fix I prototyped, and the checklist that keeps it from happening again.

Fictional teaching scenario. Rook Industries is not a real company.

Executive summary

Four responders went quiet.
Release 4.2 is why, and nobody could see it.

A shorter ping timer made more pings expire unanswered. The scoring rule then counted each expiry as a refusal, so four responders sank to the bottom of the ranking and stopped getting work. The short version

What happened with 4.2, in plain words

4.2 shipped two changes at once. One made pings expire faster. The other leaned the ranking toward distance and away from track record. Then an old scoring rule turned every expired ping into a mark against the responder, and four people could not climb out.

1 · ChangePing wait cut from 90s to 60s. Distance weighted up (0.45 → 0.60), acceptance history down (0.40 → 0.25).
2 · Misses jumpMissed pings 2.3% → 18% across all 15 areas. First-choice pings missed 2.7% → 21.4%.
3 · Misses cost the same as a "no"A missed ping and a turned-down ping both take 0.12 off the score. A taken ping adds only 0.08.
4 · Ranked lastLow score means rarely first choice, so few chances to earn points back. Scores never decay.
5 · SilenceFour responders drop from about 12 pings a week to 1–6. Handlers file tickets. Nobody sees the pattern.

What the evidence supports

  • Misses rose everywhere right after the release, so the shorter wait is the leading suspect. The gap to the next ping after a miss moved from about 92s to 62s.
  • The four were not habitual decliners: 21.6% turned down before vs 20.9% for everyone else.
  • The 4.2 weighting applies to everyone alike. Nothing in the code targets decliners.
  • "Seasonal" does not hold up: weekly callouts fell only 10–20%, while misses rose about 8×.

What I still cannot claim

  • Why the four missed about 4× more than others (58.9% vs 13.9% after 4.2). The trigger is unknown; signal or device is a guess.
  • Whether scores persist across restarts or live only in memory (Wen Li).
  • Whether the weighting change was meant to target decliners (open since 14 Aug).
  • The Vesper replay assumes a starting score near 1.0 and persistent scores.

Module 1 · Origin story

Start by asking how the system is meant to work, then force hypotheses

Before blaming anything, I asked how Dispatch accounts for terrain, weather and signal. Then I stopped Claude from piling on data and demanded a short list I could test.

Prompt 1 + Prompt 3 combined
How are the geographic (mountainous, water), climate (winter, ice, heat), environment (urban, rural), population (scattered, dense), and technology (cell service, internet availability) differences accounted for with the dispatch system? Explain at a level a 5th grader can understand. …You are providing A LOT of data but I want you to come up with 3-5 hypotheses about what went wrong with the 4.2 release.
Why it's strong: a plain-language question exposed what the system ignores. The second prompt turned data overload into testable claims.
Finding it produced

The database holds no terrain, signal, tags or population data. Only 15 areas, one responder each (Eastgate has two). Dispatch's fixed ping wait cannot adapt to a responder out of signal. Ticket 3014 (14 Jul, before 4.2) already showed this.

Three hypotheses came out of it. I have since scored each one against evidence:

HypothesisWhere it ended upHow I tested it
1 · The 60s wait causes the missesLeading, not provenGap to next ping after a miss: 92s → 62s. Misses jumped in all 15 areas. No answer-time column, so not fully testable.
2 · New ranking starves weaker-history respondersMostly contradictedReplay: the four had strong scores (0.84–1.0, about 80% taken) before 12 Aug. Their misses came first.
3 · The two feed each otherPartly supportedMisses came first, then pings fell week by week (about 49, 16, 6, 3 for the four combined).

Module 3 · Rewind

Put the first ping and the second ping side by side

Prompt 11 + Prompt 12 combined
What would need to happen for a person who has gone quiet to start getting pinged again? The declines don't seem to be the issue, as you said the declines are being logged well within the 60 second time allowance. So it maybe to do with missed and the second person who is pinged if the first person ignores/misses? …Tell me what is the correlation and percentages of taken/missed/declined when the responder is the first choice or the second (or beyond) choice. Why it's strong: it used a fact from the previous answer (declines take 6–40s) to rule out a cause, then proposed a mechanism and asked for the exact cut that would test it.
Finding it produced

The damage sits on the first ping. First-choice pings missed went from 2.7% to 21.4%. For the four, 70% of first pings after 12 Aug were missed and only 7.5% were taken (40 pings). They were first choice on 28% of callouts before 4.2 and 9% after. The ping wait is the same 60s for second and third pings.

Declines were answered in 6–40s (average about 22s) both before and after, so slow answering does not explain the misses. That revised my earlier guess.

Module 4 · X-ray vision

Reading the code settled the question the wiki could not

Prompt 1 (answers the engineering manager's open question)
Here is a question from the engineering manager: Quick one, did the change to who gets asked first apply to responders who'd already been turning jobs down, or just new ones? Can't tell from the code and don't want to guess on this. Anyone know? The pings table on rook-database has last session's numbers. Read that alongside this code. Does it support the hypotheses we have defined, and does it help to answer why the pings have totally fallen off for the four? I feel like we are dancing around the same themes but there is no clear answer of the root cause and an obvious solution. And does that answer the engineering manager's question above?
Why it's strong: it joins two sources (code and live data), asks for a verdict on my own hypotheses, admits we were going in circles, and answers a real stakeholder question in the same pass.
Findings it produced

Marcus's question answered: the 4.2 weighting applies to every responder alike. There is no tenure or decline check, and new people start at 0.5. It cut acceptance weight 0.40 → 0.25 and raised proximity 0.45 → 0.60.

The root cause has two parts. The trigger (the four's pings went unanswered about 4× as often) is unknown. The amplifier is known and lives in history.py.

Only two places in the code change a score: record_declined and record_accepted. No decay, reset or forgiveness. A "2019 TODO" for decay was never built. Break-even is about 60% taken; a near-perfect score hits 0 after about 8 non-yes pings.

Module 5 · Super speed

From finding to fix: a one-page brief and a clickable prototype

Prompt 10 + Prompt 11 combined
i don't even see the unreda badge? what is the ranking score bar trying to achieve and what can the handler do about it? …when the handler sends a message to responder, the responder needs a way to acknowledge/reply. Agree - remove the ranking score bar. What else is missing - or is extraneous - to enable clear comms and visibility to issues with the ability to action by both parties? tell me your suggestions before updating the prototype.
Why it's strong: I asked "what is this for and what can the user do about it?", a test any element must pass. Then I made Claude propose before it built.
Artifact it produced

Design decisions that came out of those two prompts:

  • Handlers never see the ranking score. They cannot change it, so fresh start is their lever.
  • The thread is two-way with Sent / Delivered / Seen.
  • Shared issue states: Flagged → Being looked at → Fix applied → Back to normal.
  • Responders can self-test, set away (pauses the flag) and escalate to support after two failed tests.
  • Resolved threads collapse to one line and move to history. The fresh-start entry is kept permanently in the routing-override audit log.

The brief, one page (05-super-speed/brief.md)

Primary user: the responder (Vesper). Secondary: the handler (Aunt Dot, Kip).

  • Responders: half-time reminder, late-claim window, delivery receipts, plain "you've been quiet" notices.
  • Handlers: a Quiet flag ("3 of last 6 pings had no answer") with one-tap check connection, fresh start and message.
  • Routing (illustrative, Wen Li and Marcus to size): miss −0.04 vs decline −0.12, undelivered ping free, idle score drifts to 0.5, guaranteed offer after 7 quiet days on non-urgent callouts, one-time reset for the four.

The fix, live

Try the prototype: handler console on the left, responder phone on the right

This is my actual clickable prototype (05-super-speed/prototype.html), embedded in full. It uses sample data for Meteor Mite and their handler Kip, one of the four who went quiet.

Try this in about two minutes

  1. Start with Meteor Mite, one of the four, on the left. Note the Quiet pill, "2 pings this week (usual 12)" and the last six pings as dots (green taken, amber turned down, red no answer).
  2. Press Check connection, then Message responder. Switch to the phone on the right and reply or acknowledge as the responder.
  3. Try Test my phone, I'll be away and Contact Kip on the phone. Untick the signal box in Demo controls to see a failed test and the escalate to support step.
  4. Press Request a fresh start. It asks to confirm and is recorded in the audit log.
  5. Compare with The Gale (Healthy, 71 pings a week) just below. Use Simulate: 3 pings taken to watch recovery, and Reset demo to start over.

What it demonstrates against the problem

  • Silence is visible within days, not weeks.
  • Missed vs declined are shown separately, and "no answer, opened" is told apart from "never opened". Responder actions show in the handler's activity log.
  • A way back in for the responder (self-test, message, away) and for the handler (fresh start).
  • Two-way thread with Sent / Delivered / Seen, cleared to history after it resolves.

Module 6 · Sidekicks

I turned my review habit into a reusable skill

Prompt 1 + Prompt 8 combined
I want to save a habit as something I can reuse. When I review a brief before it goes any further, here's what I actually check for: - It names who owns it * It clearly defines the user persona(e) and the problem we are trying to solve - It says how we'll know it worked - The scope at the end matches the scope at the start - It explains the problem before it proposes a fix * Any person could pick up the brief and understand it (i.e. not too technical or specific) Turn that into a skill called review-checklist, so I can point it at any brief and get the same check every time, without me explaining it again. …Schedule review-checklist to run every Monday morning, and let me know what it finds. Nothing needs to be ready for it to fire today. I'm setting the habit, not waiting on the result.
Why it's strong: I wrote down my own judgement as criteria (including the one that tests my own work: "any person could pick it up"), then asked for it to run without me.
Artifact it produced

.claude/skills/review-checklist/SKILL.md: six checks, each marked Pass / Partial / Fail with a quote as evidence. Any Fail means Not ready; only Partials means Ready with fixes. A scheduled task runs it every Monday at about 9:14am.

First real result: it caught my own brief

brief.md → Not ready. Jargon fails check 6; the owner is a role, not a name; the summary previews the fix; time-to-accept baseline reads "Ravi to supply"; the summary scope is narrower than the solution.

A second brief (brief_b.md) was also Not ready for different reasons: code references, the undefined word "hero", a plan stated before the problem.

Making sure it does not happen again

How the checklist would have forced the 4.2 questions earlier

I have not seen the 4.2 brief itself, so this is what each check would have asked, not a claim about what was missing. Each check lines up with something that went wrong.

1
OwnerEvery Q3 roadmap item was owned by one person, Priya, for 14 months. When she left with no overlap, nobody owned the open questions.Asks: who is the named person, and who covers them?
2
Users and problemFour responders were hidden inside an average. We had heard only from handlers, never a quiet responder.Asks: who exactly is affected? Name them.
3
Problem before fix"Mostly seasonal, don't revert" was a fix-or-not call with no numbers behind it. The data showed callouts fell 10–20%, not enough to explain it.Asks: where is the evidence of the problem?
4
Success measureAcceptance rate is the headline metric but averages hid the four. Nobody had a per-responder or "gone quiet" measure.Asks: what number moves, from what baseline?
5
Scope matchesTwo changes shipped as one, and a roadmap line "ping timeout tuning" was never confirmed as the whole item. Availability Confidence also slipped while still showing Committed.Asks: is what ships what was agreed?
6
Readable by anyoneNobody had written down how ping ranking works. Only Wen Li knew. It took code reading to answer Marcus.Asks: could a new PM follow this alone?

The loop that keeps it running

WriteBrief in one page
CheckRun review-checklist
WeeklyMonday 9:14am scheduled run

Same six questions every time, so quality does not depend on who is reviewing or how rushed they are.

Honest limits of the skill

  • It reviews a brief, not code or a release. It would not by itself have caught the miss and decline penalty being identical.
  • It judges only what is on the page, by design.
  • The scheduled run only fires while the app is open, and I have not yet seen its first run.
  • Proposed addition: a 7th check for "changes that ship together are separable, with a guardrail metric and rollback trigger", which is the exact failure in 4.2.
